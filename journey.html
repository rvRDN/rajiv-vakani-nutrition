Journey

How I arrived at this work,
and what made me take it seriously.

Long before I studied nutrition formally, I was drawn to the kinds of questions that resist simple answers.

Why do intelligent people look at the same information and reach different conclusions?

Why do traditions that have existed for generations sometimes align with modern science and sometimes seem to contradict it?

Why does advice in a field like nutrition shift so often, yet certain patterns appear again and again?

Recognizing the Pattern

For years, there were small moments that didn’t seem connected at the time. Teaching Pathshala. Helping friends understand nutrition and watching them make meaningful changes to their lives. Visiting my brother and seeing patients stop him just to say thank you. Conversations I kept returning to long after they had ended.

Individually, none of these experiences pointed toward a different life. Together, they revealed a pattern I was slow to recognize.

I kept being drawn to situations where understanding something clearly could help someone else make a better decision.

At the same time, I was beginning to question whether the work I was doing was the work I most wanted to spend my life doing. The work mattered, and many people genuinely benefited from it. But over time I found myself becoming less interested in questions of accumulation and more interested in questions of health, behavior, and what actually contributes to a good life. The questions that held my attention were changing, and eventually it became difficult to ignore where they were leading.

Nutrition wasn’t the destination from the beginning.

It was where the pattern eventually led.

Why Nutrition

Of all the places the pattern could have led, why nutrition?

By the time I decided to study nutrition formally, the decision felt less like a beginning and more like a recognition. I had been reading the research, applying what I learned, and answering questions friends brought to me for years. The interest was already there. The formal study came later.

What kept pulling me back is something specific about food itself.

Almost nothing else in a person’s life shows up as often, or matters in as many ways, as what they eat. Food is daily. It is lifelong. It is bound up in health, family, culture, identity, routine, celebration, stress, memory, and the basic question of how a person spends their day.

It’s also one of the most confused subjects in public conversation. People are surrounded by conflicting advice, oversimplified rules, aggressive marketing, and confident claims that turn out to be less certain than they sound. The result, for many people, is a slow loss of confidence in their own judgment about something that should feel ordinary.

Better understanding doesn’t fix that by itself. But it can give a person a more honest sense of what is known, what is uncertain, what is marketing, and what is actually worth changing. It can return a small amount of agency to someone who has been told, in a hundred small ways, that they don’t know what they’re doing in their own kitchen.

That’s what made nutrition feel worth taking seriously, and worth taking time on. Not because it offered clean answers, but because the people it most affects are everyone, and the help they most need is rarely a list of rules.

In New York, you can work as a nutritionist without pursuing the longer Registered Dietitian pathway. I chose the longer path. Not for the letters, and not because credentials alone would make me more credible. I chose it because I wanted to be better at the work: to understand mechanism rather than repeat recommendation, to evaluate evidence rather than relay it, and to eventually be able to work with people who rely on insurance to access care.

The point wasn’t the degree.

It was to understand the field well enough that the advice I give holds up under questioning, including my own.

The Work

Most of what I’ve learned about helping people understand difficult ideas, I didn’t learn in a classroom.

I’ve been teaching Pathshala for years. The students are young, the material is old, and the work is to make ideas that have existed for generations matter to a teenager sitting in front of you on Friday evenings. You can prepare carefully and still watch a lesson land in the wrong place, and you learn quickly that the gap is rarely about the material. It’s about the translation.

Understanding something yourself and helping someone else understand it are two very different skills.

Helping someone else understand something carries a responsibility.

The standard has to apply to my own thinking first.

Information is the beginning.

Helping a person use it well is the rest.

What I’m currently sitting with:

The more I study nutrition, the less convinced I am that facts alone change minds. I’ve watched evidence persuade some people and bounce harmlessly off others. I’ve watched stories succeed where data failed. What I’m working through isn’t what people should believe. It is how people arrive at what they believe in the first place.
