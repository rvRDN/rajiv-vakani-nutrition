What Gardening Changed About How I Think About Food

Observation June 8, 2026 Draft

A draft in progress. This page exists so the observation has a home in the Library while the piece is being written.

I did not start growing vegetables to learn about food. I started because the yard was there.

A few seasons in, a small list of things had become visible that I had not been able to see from the grocery store. None of them are large insights. They are noticings. They are the kind of thing that slowly changes how I read the rest of the nutrition conversation, rather than something I could argue for in one paragraph.

How much food is actually time

A tomato is several months of weather, water, light, and pest luck. Most of the “cost” of a plate is time. The grocery store collapses that time into a price tag and a shelf life. Once you can see the time again, the price tag reads differently.

How variety is local

The tomato I grew is not the tomato at the store. Not in flavor, not in shelf life, not in nutrient profile, not in what it does in a salad. “Eat more tomatoes” is a different sentence depending on which tomato is in scope.

How seasonality is real

Year-round availability is a remarkable supply-chain achievement and also a slight lie. Most of what I eat is not in season most of the time. A garden makes that visible because the garden refuses to pretend otherwise.

How small farming is harder than I thought

A few square meters of vegetables are difficult. The labor and attention scale punishingly. Industrial agriculture does not feel like an obvious target after you have tried to grow a useful amount of food yourself.

What I do with the noticings

Not much, directly. I do not eat noticeably differently. But I read nutrition advice differently. Advice that ignores time, place, and season feels thinner than it used to. Advice that respects those things feels more like writing by a person.
