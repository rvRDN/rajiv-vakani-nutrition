Why Nutrition Advice Keeps Changing

Essay June 8, 2026 Draft

A draft in progress. This page exists so the article has a home in the Library while the piece is being written.

Eggs are bad. Eggs are fine. Fat is bad. Saturated fat is bad. Some saturated fat is fine. Coconut oil is healthy. Coconut oil is not. Salt is the main risk. Salt was overstated. The advice changes often enough that any single recommendation can feel arbitrary, which is part of why people stop listening.

But the churn is not arbitrary. Some of it is real progress: new studies, better instruments, better analysis. Some of it is translation noise: a single paper makes news, the news flattens it into a recommendation, the recommendation gets contradicted by the next paper that did not flatten the same way. This essay is about telling those apart.

Why nutrition is harder than other health questions

You cannot blind a diet. You cannot randomize people to twenty years of eating something. Observational studies have to ask people what they eat, which is a notoriously bad measurement. The signal to noise ratio in nutrition is structurally lower than in other health fields, and most of the field knows it.

What real progress looks like

Examples of advice that has genuinely shifted because the evidence shifted. Trans fats. Dietary cholesterol. Sodium restriction. Where the field caught up.

What translation noise looks like

Examples of advice that has shifted in the press without the underlying science shifting. The coconut oil cycle. The saturated fat cycle. The intermittent fasting cycle. Why the headlines change even when the evidence does not.

How to read any single recommendation

A working posture. Treat current advice as a snapshot. Ask whether the recommendation is supported by mechanism, observational data, and trial data, or only one of those. Ask whether the source has a track record of updating when the evidence does. Trust slow, carefully-hedged advice more than confident summaries.
