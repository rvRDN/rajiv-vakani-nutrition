Food culture and behavior

Protein, Marketing, and Trust

Thinking through a moment when a macronutrient became a lifestyle.

When did protein stop being mostly a nutrient and start being an identity?

Protein used to be the thing you were supposed to get enough of.

Now it's also a signal.

It shows up on packaging in large type. It shows up in podcasts about longevity. It shows up in the way people describe a meal they feel good about, as if the grams themselves confer seriousness. I notice this because I've spent years trying to read nutrition claims carefully, and protein is one of the few areas where the underlying science often deserves more attention than the public gives it, not less.

That combination is strange.

Most nutrition panics are built on weak evidence. Protein is different. Adequate intake really does matter for satiety, lean mass, recovery, and healthy aging, especially as people get older or train harder or eat diets that are easy to under-fuel.1 Protein costs more energy to digest than carbohydrate or fat.2 Those are modest facts, but they're real.

So when protein exploded into bars, powders, cereals, chips, coffee additives, and founder stories, I didn't file it under obvious nonsense. I filed it under worth thinking about.

Elizabeth Dunn’s New York Times reporting on the premium protein bar market was what finally made me sit down and write.3 I wasn't especially interested in whether one company had built a better bar. I was interested in what it meant that an entire category had begun speaking the language of longevity, metabolism, and optimization.

The piece is partly a business story. Founders, investors, shelf space, price points, the race to build the next dominant bar. David is in there, along with a whole category of products that look less like snacks and more like small statements about how seriously you take your body.

What stayed with me wasn't any one brand. It was the tone: optimization rather than nourishment.

These products aren't marketed the way canned tuna is marketed. They borrow language from metabolism, aging, and performance. They arrive in feeds next to conversations about mTOR, muscle preservation, and metabolic health. Sometimes that science is interesting. Sometimes it's real. It's also several steps removed from whether this particular bar is a good afternoon snack.

What the boom gets right

I want to say this plainly because the skeptical version of this essay is too easy to write.

Many people do under-eat protein, or eat it in a lumpy way that doesn't help much across the day. A lot of convenient foods are high in refined carbohydrate and low in satiety. In that world, a higher-protein snack can be a genuine improvement over a candy bar or a pastry. I've got no interest in pretending otherwise just because the packaging is annoying.

There's also a real public-health logic underneath some of the enthusiasm. Aging populations need to think harder about muscle. Weight management is easier when meals don't leave you hungry again in ninety minutes. Vegetarian diets can be perfectly healthy and still need a little more attention to protein distribution than many people give them.

So when I walk through a grocery store and see protein everywhere, part of me thinks: fine. Maybe the culture is catching up to something physiology already cared about.

But then I look at the prices, the branding, and the claims, and I think: this isn't only about physiology.

What feels different about this moment

Protein has been important for a long time. What feels new is the register.

Fat had its era. Fiber had one. Antioxidants had one. Protein now seems to be carrying a wider set of hopes: control, optimization, visible discipline, resistance to aging, membership in a smarter way of eating.

That's a lot to ask of a macronutrient.

It helps explain why the products look the way they do. The grams are large. The ingredient lists are long. The design is clean in a very intentional way. The price is high enough that buying the bar says something about you, not just about your hunger.

I don't think that makes buyers foolish. People buy food for lots of reasons, including identity, convenience, and the desire to feel like they're doing something good for themselves. I buy foods for those reasons too.

What I'm trying to understand is what happens when a legitimate nutritional concern becomes a prestige category. Because that's what protein increasingly feels like to me: a nutrient with a strong evidence base that has also become a lifestyle object.

That's unusual.

It’s not unusual for marketers to exaggerate.

Where my reading slows down

This is the part of the essay that isn't really about bars.

Most of what I know about protein metabolism these days comes from long-form health media: podcasts, newsletters, interviews, clips, explainers. That's got upsides. Complex ideas reach people who would never read a review paper. It also means education, entertainment, authority, and commerce often share the same room.

I listen to that material. I learn from it. I also know that when a trusted voice discusses amino acid sensing in one segment and a sponsored product in another, my brain doesn't always keep perfect boundaries. I doubt anyone’s does.

That's where trust gets complicated for me.

Not because every endorsement is cynical. I assume many are sincere. Because the format itself makes it harder to hear the difference between “protein matters” and “this product is the serious choice.” Once protein is culturally coded as longevity food, every protein-adjacent product gets to borrow seriousness it may not have earned.

Mechanistic language makes this worse. Work on nutrient-sensing pathways such as mTOR is genuinely interesting.4 It's also cell biology, not a shopping instruction. I've heard pathway language used in public as though it closes the case on a specific engineered food. Most of the time it doesn't even begin it.

Exercise, sleep, total intake, body composition, and decades of eating pattern likely matter more than whether your snack has 24 or 28 grams of protein. I believe that. I also know it's a much less marketable sentence.

How I think through a protein claim now

I don't have a perfect method. I've got a set of questions that help me slow down.

  1. 01 About protein?

    Or a product using protein as its credential?

  2. 02 Which question?

    Getting enough matters. This product improving long-term health is harder.

  3. 03 Who benefits?

    If I accept the claim without unpacking it.

First: is this about protein at all, or about a product using protein as its credential?

Those are related. They aren't identical. Eggs, yogurt, dal, chicken, tofu, lentils, and milk solve a large share of the actual problem for a large share of people. When a bar enters the conversation, I want to know what job it's doing that those foods can't. Sometimes the answer is convenience. That's a valid answer. I just want to hear it clearly.

Second: what question is being answered?

Does getting enough protein matter? That's one question, and the answer is often yes.

Does this particular product meaningfully improve long-term health in a way ordinary food doesn't? That's a much harder question, and the evidence behind it is usually much thinner.

When those questions collapse into one sentence, the evidence for the first does a lot of unpaid work for the second.

Third: who benefits if I accept the claim without unpacking it?

Sometimes the beneficiary is me. Sometimes it's a company selling certainty to people whose real problem may be meal structure, not protein science.

None of this requires contempt. It just takes noticing that the confidence of the message and the strength of the evidence aren't always aligned.

Where I land

I don't think the protein boom is a hoax built on nothing.

Protein matters. For many people, eating more of it is a reasonable goal. For some, a bar or shake is a useful tool on a chaotic day.

But I don't think we're living through a sudden scientific discovery that explains the size of the category. We're living through a cultural moment in which protein became a symbol of seriousness about health at exactly the time many people were looking for something concrete to do about aging, weight, and uncertainty.

Markets respond to that kind of hunger quickly. Science moves more slowly.

That gap is where marketing thrives. It's also where trust frays, not because everyone is lying, but because the same word, protein, is being asked to mean too many things at once: nutrient, product, performance strategy, longevity signal, proof that you're paying attention.

I still care about the first meaning.

I'm trying to get better at noticing when the others have quietly taken over the conversation.
