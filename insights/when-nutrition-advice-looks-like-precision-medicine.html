I opened a few rows. Not to grade the whole list. Just to see if the evidence behind each pairing looked the same once I left the graphic.

It didn't. On the graphic, they still look equal.

Tomato · reflux · avoid

On the graphic: settled advice for anyone with reflux.

When you look closer Clinics often talk about tomato as a possible trigger. Advice is usually: if it bothers you, cut back and see. The graphic makes it sound like a rule for everyone.

Chocolate · migraine · avoid

On the graphic: same shape, same certainty as tomato.

What studies show Real for some people. Mixed when researchers try to prove it. Same look as tomato on the list. Weaker as a blanket rule.

Pomegranate · constipation · avoid

On the graphic: same font, same sure tone.

What's hard to find Harder to find support for this pairing. Many public notes talk about pomegranate helping digestion, not worsening constipation. Same certainty on the list. Much thinner underneath.

Same image. Different reading.

You're looking at the same eight rows. They no longer look like eight equally solid clinical facts. They look like a format that needs every row to seem equally sure.

If the list marked strong rows and weak rows, it'd be harder to save and share. The design works better when everything looks settled.

None of this means food-and-condition advice is fake. Real personalization exists. Absolute rules exist for some conditions. Clinics run protocols with clear who-it's-for and follow-up.

What those share isn't just being specific. They share a loop:

  • Who is this for?
  • How do you know it applies?
  • What dose or form?
  • What should change if it doesn't help?

Real personal advice includes a way to check and adjust. The graphic gives you the avoid rule without the test. It ends where the experiment would begin.

The next time a graphic like this shows up in your feed, where do you look first?

Not whether to ignore it. Some rows may still help someone notice a real trigger. The better question is whether the advice is truly personal, or only looks that way.

  1. Does it name who it is for, or only a condition label?
  2. Does it tell you to test it: remove, watch, put back, adjust?
  3. If every row looks equally sure, is that about the foods, or about the format?
