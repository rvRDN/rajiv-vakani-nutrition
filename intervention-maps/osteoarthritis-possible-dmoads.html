Is there a drug that changes osteoarthritis itself?

Bottom line

Some medicines change a disease, not only the pain. Rheumatoid arthritis has options in that class. Osteoarthritis does not currently have an approved one. Investigated candidates are real research, not a hidden first conversation. One widely watched knee injection showed modest pain at some time points without a clear improvement in function or in the joint on imaging. Rheumatoid arthritis medicines are not the treatment for hand osteoarthritis.

What “changing the disease” means

Pain relief and disease modification are different jobs. A cream, a pill, an exercise program, or a steroid shot can ease symptoms without proving the joint itself was repaired or slowed.

Medicines that change the underlying disease, not only the pain, are called disease-modifying treatments. This page exists because people ask whether osteoarthritis has one. The honest answer is that the class is not established here.

What has been investigated

Lorecivivint is a knee injection studied as a possible disease-changing medicine. A 2026 review of six trials, 2,179 people, found modest improvement on a common osteoarthritis pain score at some time points. It did not find a clear improvement in function, in other pain measures, or in the joint on imaging. Side effects looked similar to placebo. Modest pain without function or structure is not an approved disease-modifying drug.

What this is not

Disease-modifying medicines used for rheumatoid arthritis have not established a treatment role in hand osteoarthritis.

Weight-loss medicines can move weight, pain, and function without proving the joint itself changed. That is a different page.

Glucosamine and stem-cell injections are also not this class.

What taking part usually involves

  • For investigated injections: a research or specialty setting, not a standard prescription.
  • Follow-up that should ask about pain, function, and imaging, not assume the disease was modified.
  • A willingness to hear that “possible” is not the same as “available and proven.”

What this does not do

This page doesn't turn osteoarthritis into rheumatoid arthritis. It also doesn't hide the question. Pain and function options still matter. An approved medicine that changes osteoarthritis itself is not one of them yet.

Ask your clinician

Useful questions

Start here: “Are we talking about easing my pain, or about a medicine that would change the joint disease itself?”

Also worth asking:

  • If a clinic offers a “cartilage drug,” what exactly was shown besides pain?
  • Is this approved for osteoarthritis, or still investigational?
  • If I have hand osteoarthritis, are rheumatoid arthritis medicines being borrowed from a different disease?
