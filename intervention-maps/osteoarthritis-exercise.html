Does exercise help? Is the hip the same as the knee?

Bottom line

Guidelines strongly recommend exercise for osteoarthritis of the knee and the hip. The average change in pain is modest, and it isn't a guaranteed change you would notice. The hip has much less research than the knee, and the average hip benefit may be smaller. That doesn't make exercise optional wellness, and it doesn't mean the knee numbers apply to the hip. Exercise hasn't been shown to rebuild cartilage or delay joint replacement.

What this is

The evidence is about planned, repeated exercise programs, often land-based, and often with some structure or supervision. It is not about being told to “move more.”

Guidelines put that kind of exercise in usual osteoarthritis care. How large the average benefit is, and how that differs at the knee versus the hip, is a separate question.

Knee

What the knee evidence shows

Guidelines strongly recommend exercise for knee osteoarthritis.

A large Cochrane review of land-based exercise found that pain and function improved on average. The size of that improvement depended on what exercise was compared with. Against a placebo-like or attention comparison, average pain improvement was about 9 points on a 0 to 100 scale. Against usual care, it was about 13 points.

The reviewers themselves treated a change of about 12 points as the kind of pain difference a person would typically notice. On that measure, the average benefit is uncertain: it may or may not be large enough for a typical person to feel a clear difference.

The review did not find a reliable ranking of one exercise type over another, and it did not show that more sessions automatically work better. It also did not show that exercise restores cartilage.

Same treatment family. Different evidence.

Hip

What the hip evidence shows

Guidelines also strongly recommend exercise for hip osteoarthritis. That recommendation is not a smaller copy of the knee story.

The hip review is much smaller: 18 trials rather than the knee review’s 139. Average pain improvement versus usual care was about 7 points on a 0 to 100 scale. That average may sit below a change people would typically notice. In placebo or sham comparisons, the evidence for pain relief becomes less clear.

The honest public sentence is the same one the hip review supports: exercise remains recommended, and people should be told the average benefit may be modest.

Don't use the knee trial counts, or the knee averages, as if they were hip evidence.

What taking part usually involves

A real program asks someone to start and repeat movement in a painful joint. Time, flares, transport, a safe place to train, and whether any coaching is available all shape whether a study program can happen in ordinary life.

“Exercise is free” isn't an honest description for everyone. A one-time instruction to “exercise more” is not the same as the structured, repeated programs studied in these reviews.

  • Repeated sessions over weeks, not a one-time tip.
  • Progression that can be adjusted when pain flares.
  • A form you can actually begin and continue: walking, strength work, cycling, or a mixed program.

There isn’t a proven best type for everyone. The useful program is the one a person can start and keep doing.

Canes, braces, and splints

Canes, selected braces, and hand splints can help with daily function. They're related practical supports, not another kind of exercise, and they don't inherit the exercise evidence above.

Most of the trial evidence on this page is about the knee and the hip. Hand osteoarthritis often uses splints in usual care. That is a different decision from a knee or hip exercise program.

What this does not do

Exercise hasn't been shown to rebuild the joint or delay replacement. Trials of joint replacement enrolled people who were already surgical candidates. Those results don't mean trying exercise first was a waste, and they don't rewrite this page.

If weight management is relevant for you, that's a separate decision. Combined programs exist. They're still two jobs.

Ask your clinician

Useful questions

Start here: “Given that my problem is in my [knee / hip], what kind of exercise program is realistic for me, and how will we tell if it’s helping?”

Also worth asking:

  • Is a supervised program available, or is home exercise the plan?
  • How should I adjust when the joint flares?
  • Would a cane, brace, or splint help a specific daily task?
  • If replacement has already been discussed, how does exercise still fit?
