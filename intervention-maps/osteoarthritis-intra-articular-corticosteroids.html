How long does a steroid shot last?

Bottom line

A steroid injection can ease knee pain for a short time. Help is usually greatest in the first couple of weeks, smaller by about three months, with no clear benefit at six months. In a two-year trial, repeated triamcinolone injections every 12 weeks caused more cartilage loss than saline, without better pain relief. Pain relief and what happens to the joint are different questions. This is not disease control.

What this is

A clinician injects a corticosteroid into the joint for short-term pain relief. Guidelines still allow that as a short-term option for the knee.

It isn't a gel shot, not PRP, and not a stem-cell injection. Those are separate pages.

How long the pain help lasts

A Cochrane review of knee injections found the benefit is most plausible at one to two weeks. By about 13 weeks it is smaller. At 26 weeks there is no clear benefit.

The evidence quality is low, and larger trials tended to show smaller effects. That's a reason for modest expectations, not a reason to call the shot a lasting osteoarthritis treatment.

Repeating shots on a schedule is a different finding

In a two-year trial, people with knee osteoarthritis received triamcinolone 40 mg or saline every 12 weeks. The steroid group lost more cartilage thickness. Pain was not better than saline.

That finding is about repeated triamcinolone on a fixed schedule for two years. It isn't a general rule that any second injection, ever, will do the same thing. It also isn't an argument that a single short-term shot is secretly rebuilding or secretly destroying the joint in the same way.

Both facts belong together: a brief pain tool, and a repeated-use structural cost that has been measured.

Knee, hip, and hand

The duration map and the two-year cartilage trial are knee research. Hip and hand injections shouldn't inherit those results as if they had been proven there.

What taking part usually involves

  • A clinic visit, a needle into the joint, and a short period of relative rest afterward.
  • A plan for what “helped” would look like over days and weeks, not months as a default.
  • A conversation about whether this is a one-time flare tool or the start of a calendar of shots.

What this does not do

A steroid shot hasn't been shown to control osteoarthritis as a disease. Feeling better at two weeks is not proof the joint was repaired.

See also hyaluronic acid, PRP, and stem cells. Four shots, four decisions.

Ask your clinician

Useful questions

Start here: “If we do a steroid shot, how long should I expect it to help, and how will we decide whether to repeat it?”

Also worth asking:

  • Is this meant as a short flare tool, or as a regular schedule?
  • What do we know, and not know, about cartilage if shots are repeated?
  • Is my painful joint the knee, or are we borrowing knee evidence for a hip or hand?
  • How does this sit beside creams and pills or exercise?
